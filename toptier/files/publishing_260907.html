Do not delete anything yet

A published page has an address, and other things point at that address. Google's index. Your own menus. Another page on your site. A Facebook post from 2024. The moment the page is gone, every one of those pointers leads a real person to a page that says the page does not exist. That is not a tidy website, that is four broken doors, and search engines take months to stop knocking.

So the order is: find out what points at them, decide where those people should go instead, set the forwarding up, then delete. Deleting is the last move, not the first.

What a post on a roofing company's website is actually for

Not news. Not a diary. It is the answer to a question, sitting where the question gets asked.

Nobody subscribes to a roofer's blog. Nobody checks it on a Tuesday to see what is new. So a post written as an announcement has no audience and never will. That is why most contractor blogs die after four posts, which is exactly what happened to yours.

A post earns its keep a different way. Somebody types a question into a search engine, or asks it on your estimate appointment, and your post is the page that answers it. That page then does three things at once, forever, without you present:

What it doesHow that actually happens
It gets foundSearch engines match pages to questions. A page that answers one specific question, in the words a homeowner would use, is matchable. A page that says "welcome to our blog" is not.
It proves you know the tradeThe person reading it is deciding whether to call you. A straight, unhedged answer to their question does more than any amount of description of how experienced you are.
It saves you the conversationYour crew answers the same questions on every job. Once it is written down you can send the link before the appointment instead of covering it again at the door.

What makes it sound like you and not like a machine

  • It answers the question in the first two sentences. Not after four paragraphs of introduction. If somebody asked how long a roof takes, the number goes at the top.
  • It uses your actual numbers and your actual practice. How many people are on your crew. What you tarp. What brand of shingle you install. Those are facts nobody can copy from another site, and they are the reason the page reads as real.
  • It names the places. Albertville, St Michael, Otsego, Monticello, Buffalo. Not "the Twin Cities metro area."
  • It admits something. "Your home is going to be a construction zone for a day" is a sentence that buys more trust than three paragraphs of reassurance, and it is already on your own pillars sheet.
  • It does not use words you do not use. If you would not say "utilize" or "solutions" or "seamless" out loud on a driveway, they do not belong in something published under your name.

The verb test

Roofing verbs. inspect, document, measure, replace, repair

Claim handling verbs. file, handle, advocate, negotiate, ensure, cover

In Minnesota, doing claim work without a chapter 72B adjuster license carries penalties of up to $10,000 per violation. A post that says the company will handle your insurance claim is a licensing problem, not a copywriting problem.

The approved way to put it: we cannot help you with your policy, but we can help you with your roof.

That is the whole rule. Use the first list, keep off the second, and if a sentence needs a verb from the second list to work, the sentence is describing something else.

You may not ask for specific review content

Google's rule, live since 16 February 2026: you may not ask a customer to mention a particular thing in a review. Not a staff member's name, not the service they bought, not the town they live in.

It bans the ask, not the review. If a customer writes "Guy came out to Otsego and did the whole roof in a day," that review is perfect and nothing is wrong with it. What you may not do is ask for it in those terms.

This bites here because ending a post with a request for reviews is the most natural close in the world. "If we replaced your roof, we would love a review mentioning your town" is exactly the sentence the rule prohibits. "If we replaced your roof, we would appreciate a review" is fine.

Two more that apply to everything published

  • The license number goes on advertising. Minnesota 326B.87 requires the contractor license number on advertising. Yours is BC807923 and it is already in the footer of every page of the site, so a blog post inherits it. Worth knowing so it never gets removed.
  • Never say or imply that insurers drop homeowners over roof age. It is not true in Minnesota and it is the single easiest thing to write by accident when a post is about roof age.
Hand these to your Claude as a standing rule for your project so you are not re-explaining them on every draft.

Which photographs earn a place

The bar is not quality. The bar is whether it proves something a sentence cannot.

A website with forty photographs of roofs is not more persuasive than one with eight. It is less, because forty means nobody chose. Eight means somebody chose, and the visitor can feel the difference without being able to name it.

A photograph earns its place ifWhat it proves
It is a before and an after of the same house, same angle, same time of dayThe single most persuasive image a roofer can own. Nothing else in marketing does this job.
Your crew is in it, working, with faces and shirts visibleThat the work is done by people who work for you, rather than by whoever was available that week.

Naming them so they can be found

Two pieces of text per photograph, and both of them are a machine's job.

A photograph arrives off a camera called something like DSC07758.jpg or IMG_4471.jpeg. Uploaded like that, it is invisible: no search engine has any idea what it shows, and neither does anyone using a screen reader. Two things fix that.

WhatWhat it is forShape
The file name Search engines read it. It is also how you find the file again in two years. Lower case, words separated by hyphens, no spaces, no underscores. What it shows, then where. metal-roof-replacement-otsego-mn.jpg
The alternative text What a screen reader says out loud, and what search engines use to understand the image. Set inside WordPress, not in the file. One plain sentence describing what is in the frame. "A finished dark gray metal roof on a single story home in Otsego, Minnesota."
Alternative text is a description, not a slogan. "Top Tier Roofing best roofer Albertville MN roofing company" is the thing everybody does and it is worse than leaving it blank. Describe the picture. That is all it is for.
