The Review Ask, Rewritten

Google changed what a business is allowed to ask a customer to write. The wording below is current and safe to send today. Anything on file that lists things to ask a customer to mention is out of date and should be replaced with this.

Read this first, in one paragraph

You may ask any customer for a review. You may not ask them what to put in it. Not a person's name, not the type of work, not the town, not a star count. That is the whole change. The reviews you already have are fine, and a customer who writes "Keith was great" on their own has done nothing wrong. The rule governs your ask, not their words.

1. The rule, in Google's own words

From Google's Rating Manipulation policy, article 7400114, in effect since February 16, 2026:

Google, verbatim

"When soliciting reviews, merchants should not require or pressure users to leave ratings or write reviews while on the premises, nor should they request that specific content be included."

Google, verbatim, listed as a violation

"Merchants requesting that staff solicit reviews that include specific content, including content that identifies a staff member."

Read it here: https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114

Two things worth pulling out of that text. First, "specific content" is broader than a person's name. A service and a town are specific content too, which is the trap, because "ask them to mention the roof and the city" is the single most common piece of review advice on the internet and it is now a violation. Second, the words are "require or pressure." Asking is allowed. Standing there waiting while they type it is a different thing.

What is not affected

  • Your existing reviews are safe. The policy governs the ask, and there is no retroactive rule. Nothing already posted needs to be touched.
  • A customer naming somebody on their own violates nothing. If they write about the crew, the town, or the roof because that is what they remember, that is a genuine experience, which is exactly what the policy is protecting.
  • Your replies to reviews are not solicitations. Writing back to a posted review is a response, not an ask, and the rule above does not reach it.

No enforcement mechanism is published, so nobody outside Google can tell you what the penalty is or how it is detected. Treat that as a reason to stay clean rather than a reason to gamble.

2. The four asks, ready to send

Same rule behind all four: name the company, name nothing else. Every one of these invites the customer to write whatever they actually remember, which is both compliant and, as it turns out, what a stranger reading the profile finds credible.

A. Text message

Text

Thanks again for trusting us with your home. If you have a minute, would you tell us about your experience with Top Tier on Google? It helps a small local crew more than anything. [review link]

Why it is worded this way: "tell us about your experience" hands the customer a blank page instead of a checklist, so nothing specific is being requested. "If you have a minute" and the absence of any star count keep it from reading as pressure.

B. Email

Subject line

A quick favor from Top Tier

Body

Thanks for having us out. We are a small crew and most of our work comes from people who took the time to say something about us publicly.

If you are willing, would you tell us about your experience with Top Tier on Google? Whatever you write is up to you, good or bad, and it takes about a minute.

Here is the link: [review link]

Thanks either way.
Top Tier Roofing and Remodeling
(612) 205-5503

Why it is worded this way: "good or bad" is doing real work. Google separately bans selectively soliciting positive reviews, so an ask that is open to an unhappy customer is safer than one that is not, and it also costs you nothing, because an unhappy customer usually just does not answer.

C. Out loud, at the job

Spoken

If we did what we said we would do, would you say so on Google? Whatever you want to write. It is the only place people who do not know us can check.

Why it is worded this way: "whatever you want to write" is the compliance clause, spoken. "If we did what we said we would do" invites a no, and an ask that can be declined is not pressure. One caution from the policy text itself: the ban on pressuring somebody "while on the premises" means the ask is fine, but handing a customer a phone and waiting is not the same as asking.

3. What you must never ask for

These are the exact phrasings you will find if you search the internet for review advice. Every one of them was standard practice until February 2026, and every one of them is now a request for specific content.

Do not send thisWhy it is a violation
"If Keith took care of you, mention him by name." Content that identifies a staff member. Named in the policy explicitly.
"Please mention that we replaced your roof." Requesting a service be included. Specific content.
"It helps if you mention Albertville." Requesting a town be included. Specific content, and the most common SEO advice there is.
"People usually mention how long it took and whether they knew who was on the roof." A suggestion is still a request. Softening it does not change what is being asked for.
"If we earned five stars, would you leave five stars?" Requesting a rating. Also reads as pressure.
"Let us know first if anything was wrong, and if you are happy leave a review." Gating. Selectively soliciting positive reviews is a separate, named violation.
Anything of value for a review: gift card, discount, drawing, free gutter cleaning, a bonus to a crew member per review. Incentivized reviews. This one predates the 2026 change and it is the fastest way to lose a profile.
"Tell us about your experience with Top Tier." Requests nothing specific. This is the safe form of every ask.

4. Writing your own, so you never have to ask us again

Take any review ask you are about to send and run it through this:

  1. Delete every proper noun except your own company name. No person, no town, no product, no brand.
  2. Delete every number. No star counts, no ratings, no "top rated."
  3. Delete every word that describes the work. Roof, siding, gutters, storm, insurance. If it survives without them, it is clean.
  4. Check it works for an unhappy customer. If you would not want this exact message going to somebody who was annoyed with you, it is gating.
  5. Check nothing is offered. No discount, no entry, no thank you gift.

What is left is a sentence that asks for a review and requests nothing. That is the entire method, and it is why all four asks above are so plain.
