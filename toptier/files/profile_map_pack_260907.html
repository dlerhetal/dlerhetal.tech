The Profile and the Map Pack

Top Tier Roofing & Remodeling, LLC · a hands-on walk through the Google Business Profile

This one is manual, and that is deliberate

Most of the work on this engagement can be built, checked or fixed without anybody sitting at a keyboard clicking through screens. This one cannot. The Google Business Profile console only opens for a human who is signed in to the Google account that owns the listing. There is no way to hand that off, no shared login to borrow, and nothing that can sit in that chair on your behalf.

So this guide is written differently from the others. It is literal: go here, click that, this is what the screen should say, this is what it looks like when it went wrong. Every value you discover along the way gets typed into a box on this page, and every later step fills itself in from what you typed.

Before you start

  • Who does this: one person, Guy, at a computer. Not a phone job. The profile can be edited from a phone but the categories and services screens are much easier on a full screen.
  • What you must have: a browser, and you must already be signed in to the Google account that owns the Top Tier listing. Not any Google account. The one with ownership.
  • If you are not the owner of the listing, this stops here. If the screens below show you nothing to edit, or show somebody else's business, then the listing is owned by another Google account, most likely one held by a past or present marketing vendor. Nothing further on this page can be done until whoever holds that account either transfers ownership to you or makes your account a manager. That is a phone call to a person, not a setting. Step 1 tells you exactly what that screen looks like so you can tell the difference.
  • How long: about 15 minutes to read. About 30 to 45 minutes of clicking if the profile is already yours, spread over the seven steps. The photographs step is open ended, and the two extra listings at the end are about 15 minutes each.
  • Nothing here costs money. Every item in this guide is free.

Three things not to press, and where each one bites

The forbidden button is usually the first one anybody naturally presses. These are the three on this page. Read them now, before you are in the console with the mouse moving.

  1. Do not add a town or a service word to the business name. Not "Top Tier Roofing Albertville", not "Top Tier Roofing and Siding". Google's rule is that unnecessary words in the name can get the whole profile suspended, and the profile is what feeds the map pack. The name box is the easiest field on the screen to type in, which is exactly the problem. Go to the name step
  2. Do not delete a category to make room. Categories are additive. If a category is wrong, the fix is to change the primary and add the right additional ones. Deleting the wrong one silently removes the searches you were being shown for, and nothing on screen tells you what you lost. Go to the categories step
  3. Do not put a phone number in a profile post. Google calls it phone stuffing and it is explicitly prohibited. The post gets a "Call now" button that uses the verified number anyway. This bites hardest the week the mail lands, because a phone number is exactly what you want to type. Go to the posts step

Why this is worth an afternoon

The map pack is the three businesses Google shows above the ordinary results when somebody searches for a roofer near them. It is the most valuable free space in local search.

Google names exactly three things that decide who appears there: relevance, meaning how well the profile matches what was searched, distance from the searcher, and prominence, which is largely reviews. [vendor] Distance you cannot change. Reviews are a separate piece of work already under way. Relevance is what this page is about, and it is the part nobody looks at.

"There's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking on Google."
Google Business Profile Help, local ranking

There is one hard date attached to this

Drop 1 of the direct mail campaign was released on 7 September 2026 and lands in homes between 15 and 22 September 2026, in Albertville, St Michael, Otsego, Monticello and Buffalo. A person who gets a postcard and is mildly curious looks the company up before they call. What they find is the profile and the map pack. That is the only deadline on this page.

Step 1. Get into the profile, and prove it is the right one

Two minutes. The point of this step is not to change anything. It is to find out whether you can.

Do this

  1. Open a browser and go to the Business Profile manager: https://business.google.com/
  2. Look at the top right corner of the screen. There is a circle with a letter or a photograph in it. That is the Google account you are signed in as. Click it and read the email address.
  3. Type that email address in here so the rest of this page knows which account did the work:
    If you are signed in as more than one account, this is the one showing at the top of the account list when the profile appears.
  4. You should now see Top Tier listed. Open it.
What success looks like. The business appears with editable buttons across the top of it: Edit profile, Read reviews, Messages, Photos, and so on. You can click Edit profile and the fields are live, not grayed out.
What failure looks like, and what each kind means.
  • "Add your business to Google" and nothing else. This account does not own or manage the listing. It exists, because it shows on the public map, so it belongs to somebody else's Google account.
  • You can see the business but every field is grayed out. You have a limited role, not ownership. Whoever is the primary owner has to promote your account.
  • "This business is already claimed" with a partly hidden email address. That partial address is the clue to who holds it. Google offers a "Request access" button on that screen, which sends a message to the current owner and starts a waiting period.
If any of those three appear, this job stops here and becomes a conversation with another person. Note which one you saw in the box below and send it back using the last screen of this guide. Every remaining step on this page needs an account that can edit, and there is no way around that.

Step 2. Categories, primary and additional

This is the field nobody sees but Google. A searcher never sees it. It is the strongest ranking signal the profile has, and a wrong primary category loses the map pack quietly, with nothing on screen to tell you.

What primary and additional actually do

FieldWhat it does
Primary category You get exactly one. It is the single strongest statement the profile makes about what this business is, and it carries far more weight than any of the others. When somebody searches for a roofer, this is the field that decides whether Top Tier is even in the pool of candidates before distance and reviews sort them.
Additional categories You get several. Each one adds another kind of search the profile can be considered for. They are worth much less than the primary one individually, but they are how the other things the company does become findable at all. There is no cost to a correct additional category and no benefit to a stretched one.
The rule that governs both. Google's guidelines say plainly:
"Do not use categories solely as keywords or to describe attributes of your business."
Google Business Profile guidelines, article 3038177
In practice that means a category is picked because it is a thing the company does, from Google's fixed list. It is not a place to put words you want to rank for.

Do this. Record before you change.

  1. In the profile, click Edit profile.
  2. On the About tab, find Business category. It shows the primary category first, then any additional ones underneath it.
  3. Read what is there now and type it into these boxes before you touch anything. If a change turns out to be wrong, this is the only record of what it used to be.
  4. Now decide whether the primary is right. The test is simple and it is not a marketing question: when a stranger with a problem searches, what is the one word they use for the business that solves it? The primary category should be that. You know your customers and you know what they call you. The field is a dropdown with a fixed list, so type the first few letters and take the closest genuine match.
  5. Then look at the additional categories against the work the company actually sells. Roofing, siding, gutters, and storm and hail work are all real lines of business here, and each one has customers who search for it by its own name. Anything the company genuinely does and gets paid for is a candidate. Anything it does not do is not.
  6. Click Save.
The landmine on this screen. Do not delete a category to make room for a new one. Additional categories are additive, and there is room for several. Deleting one removes the searches the profile was being considered for, and nothing on screen reports what was lost. If something is wrong, change the primary and add what is missing. Only remove a category that describes work the company does not do.
What success looks like. The About tab shows one primary category and a short list of additional ones, every one of them a real line of business, and none of them containing a town name.
What failure looks like. The save button grays out and nothing happens, which usually means the account has a view-only role. Or the category you want is not in the list at all, which means Google does not have one for it. There is no way to add your own, and the correct move is to take the closest real match rather than a stretched one.

Step 3. The services list

After the town pages on the website, this is the highest value item on the whole walk list. It is the only other profile field Google documents as affecting what a business gets shown for, and most contractors leave it half filled in.

"When local customers search Google for an offering you provide, that service may be highlighted on your profile."
Google Business Profile Help, services, article 9455399

That sentence is the whole reason this step exists. Google makes no equivalent statement about the description, the products, or the posts. Effort spent rewriting the description is effort spent on a field with no documented effect. Effort spent here is not.

Do this

  1. In Edit profile, open the Services tab.
  2. Google offers a list of suggested services attached to your categories. Turn on every one that Top Tier genuinely does. This is the fast part and it is worth doing first, because these are the names Google already recognizes.
  3. Then add custom services for anything real that the suggested list missed. There is an Add custom service option at the bottom of the list.
  4. Name each one the way a customer would say it out loud, not the way the trade says it. The person searching is a homeowner with a problem, not a roofer.
  5. Cover all four lines of business, not just the first one. Roofing, siding, gutters, and storm and hail work each have their own customers searching their own words.
  6. Click Save.
What may not go in a custom service name. Google's own wording:
"Avoid including profanity, gibberish, personal information, prices, or phone numbers in custom service names."
Google Business Profile Help, article 9455399
So no prices in a service name, and no phone number. A price belongs on the website where it can carry its own conditions.
Do not stuff it. A services list is not a keyword box. Fifty entries that are variations of the same four things reads as manipulation, and it is the same instinct the category rule already forbids. Every service you offer, named once, in plain words. That is the whole target.
What success looks like. The Services tab lists every line of work the company sells, in words a homeowner would use, and somebody who does not work here could read the list and correctly describe what the business does.
What failure looks like. A custom service is rejected on save. The usual cause is a price, a phone number or a web address inside the name. Take it out and save again.

Step 5. The business name, and the suspension risk

Two minutes, and it protects everything in the four steps above it.

Google's own words

"Including unnecessary information in your business name isn't permitted, and could result in the suspension of your Business Profile."
Google Business Profile guidelines, article 3038177. Google's own published example of a banned name is "Auto Service Experts".

That example matters, because it is exactly the same shape as adding a service word or a town to a real name. The industry habit of writing "Top Tier Roofing Albertville" into the name box is not a clever edge. It is the specific thing the rule names, and the penalty is not a ranking demotion. It is the loss of the profile that feeds the map pack.

The real name is safe. The company's actual name, whichever of the two spellings you settled on in the previous step, is by definition permitted. It is the real world name of the business. The upside of adding an extra word is small and undocumented. The downside is losing everything.

Do this

  1. In Edit profile, About tab, look at the Business name field.
  2. Check there is no town name in it. No Albertville, no St Michael, none of the five towns the mail is going to.
  3. Check there is no service word in it beyond what is genuinely part of the company name. No added "Siding", no added "Storm Damage", no added "Contractors".
  4. Check there is no slogan, no phone number and no web address in it.
  5. If it is clean, change nothing and move on. If it is not, remove the extra words and save.
If you have to correct it, expect a delay. A change to the business name often goes into a review queue rather than taking effect immediately, and the profile can show "pending" for a while. That is normal and it is not a sign anything is wrong. [unconfirmed]

Step 6. A post, and what the thing is actually called

The place on the profile where you add a photograph, a piece of news, or something like a note about a finished job.

The name of the thing

It is called a Post. That is the word Google uses in its own help documentation and in its policies. In the current console the button is labeled "Add update", and the section is called Updates. Same feature, two names, and the mismatch is why it is hard to find when somebody tells you to "post something".

If you cannot find that exact wording, you are not in the wrong place. Depending on which account you are signed in as and whether you are in the console, in search results or on a phone, the button reads "Add update" or "Create post". Both open the same thing. [unconfirmed]

Where it lives. Open the profile and look at the row of buttons across the top: Edit profile, Read reviews, Messages, Photos, and among them Add update. That is the one. From a phone the same feature sits under a Promote or Updates heading. If the row of buttons is not visible you are on the public view of the listing rather than the console, which means going back to step 1.

Two rules that bite here, and one that follows you everywhere

1. Never put a phone number in a post. Google calls it phone stuffing:

"To avoid the risk of abuse, we do not allow your post content to include a phone number. Instead, you can attach a 'Call now' button to your post."
Google Business Profile Help, posts, article 7213077

The Call now button uses the verified number on the profile anyway, so nothing is lost. This is the single most likely mistake the week the mail lands, because the postcard has a phone number on it and the instinct is to make the post match the card.

2. Never post your own question and then answer it. Answering a real customer's question is a proper feature of the profile and it is worth doing every time one appears. Seeding your own falls under Google's fake engagement rule, which covers content posted from multiple accounts by or at the request of one person. Frequently asked questions belong on the website instead, where they are entirely yours and carry no risk at all.

3. You may not ask for specific content in a review. This one is not about posts, it is about every conversation the company has with a customer, and it is the one most likely to be violated by accident because it is standard advice everywhere else. Google prohibits a merchant asking a customer to include specific content in a review: naming a staff member, naming a service, or naming a town. That went live on 16 February 2026. It bans the ask, not the review. A customer who writes "Guy came out and did a great job" of their own accord has broken nothing, and the review is fine. Asking them to write it is the violation. So the ask is simply an ask for an honest review, with no shopping list attached.

Do this

  1. Click Add update.
  2. Write two or three plain sentences about something real. A finished job, a service now running, a seasonal note. It does not need to be clever and it does not need to sell.
  3. Attach a photograph if you have one for it.
  4. Attach the Call now button rather than typing the number.
  5. Post it.

The cadence, which matters more than any single post

A profile that posted once and stopped looks abandoned to a person reading it, which is the audience that counts here. Google documents no ranking effect from posts at all, so the reason to keep posting is the human one: somebody who just got a postcard is deciding whether this company still exists.

The one post that is worth planning. Put a post up the week the cards land, between 15 and 22 September 2026, saying the same thing the card says. Somebody who got the card and searched the name then finds a profile that matches what is in their hand. No phone number in the text. The Call now button carries it.
What success looks like. The update appears on the public listing, and there is a Call now button on it that dials the right number.
What failure looks like. The post is rejected or quietly disappears. The most common cause by a wide margin is a phone number somewhere in the text, including one written out in words or inside an image caption.

Step 7. Photographs

Real jobs, recent dates. The cheapest credibility available, and most competitors neglect it.

A profile with four stock photographs from three years ago and a profile with thirty real roofs from this season cost the same to run. The second one answers the only question the person holding a postcard is actually asking, which is whether these people do this work and whether they still do it now.

You are already collecting these. Job photographs are being captured already, so this step is not about starting something new. It is about the last few clicks that move them from where they sit to where a customer sees them. Getting those same photographs onto the website is a separate job and it is not this guide. This step is the profile only.

Do this

  1. From the profile, click Photos.
  2. Add photographs of finished work. Exteriors, whole roofs, and the house they are on.
  3. Add them in batches over time rather than fifty at once. A profile that gains a few photographs every month reads as a working company. One that gained everything on a single day two years ago reads as a company that stopped.
  4. Make sure the logo and a cover photograph are set, because those are the two the listing shows first.
The only real trap here. Do not put a phone number, a price or a web address into a photograph caption. The same rules that govern posts reach the text attached to an image.
What success looks like. The Photos tab shows recent work with recent dates, and somebody scrolling it would conclude the company is busy.
What failure looks like. An upload fails or an image is removed. Very large files and screenshots of other websites are the usual causes.

Step 8. The other two listings, Bing and Apple

Neither of these will ever match Google for volume. Both are free, both take about fifteen minutes, and both are claimed once and then left alone.

Why they are worth the half hour

  • Apple Maps is what an iPhone opens. A person who taps an address on an iPhone is not in Google Maps unless they went there deliberately. Whatever Apple holds about the business is what they see.
  • Bing feeds more than Bing. Bing's index is what sits behind Copilot's local answers, so an unclaimed Bing listing is a wrong answer waiting to be given by an assistant that a growing number of people ask instead of searching. [unconfirmed]
Use exactly the same name, address and phone you settled on in step 4. The entire value of claiming a second and third listing is that they agree with the first one. Two listings that disagree are worse than one listing on its own.
