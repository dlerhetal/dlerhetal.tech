1. The one rule that gates everything

87 of the 131 records do not have a person on them.

The contact field says Property Manager, Agency Principal, Property Owner or Association Manager. Those are job titles, not people. Nobody has that name.

You cannot email a job title. So those 87 records have to be phoned first, and the only job of that first call is to come away with a human name and a direct email address. Until that happens, the record cannot enter the email lane at all.

That is why the no-name records go to the front of the line. They are not the best prospects and they are not the worst. They are the ones that gate the most downstream work, so they get worked first. Every one you convert from a title into a person unlocks an email you could not otherwise send.

And the mechanic that makes it self-tracking

When the call gets you a name, type it over the placeholder in Contractors Cloud. That single edit is the gate. Any record still reading "Property Manager" has not been converted. Any record reading a real name has. You do not need a special field to track this one - the contact name field already is the flag.

What "done" looks like, per lane

  • Lane A is done when a record no longer says "Property Manager" - either a real name is on it, or it is closed as unreachable with the reason written down.
  • Lane B is done when a reply has arrived, or the follow-up interval you set has run out and you have decided to stop.
  • Lane C is done when an email address is on the record, or you have decided the account is worth a walk-in instead.
  • The portal lane is done per company when the registration is submitted. Acceptance is out of your hands and can take weeks. Submitted is the milestone you control.
  • A walk-in is done when someone at the building has your card and you know who the roof decision actually runs through.

Walk-in 3 visits a week, 119 eligible

Guy has committed to 3 visits a week. That is a stated number from the person doing the work, so it is the number this lane is built on. Every other volume on this page is an assumption; this one is not.

Three a week against 119 eligible records changes what this lane is. It stops being a coverage exercise and becomes a selection problem. Nobody visits 119 buildings on three a week without it running most of a year, so the lane needs a ranked queue, not a route. Section 11 builds it, ranks all 119, and gives a standing "next three" that updates itself.

A walk-in is still a second touch. It follows a call, it does not replace one.

Portal block 8 verified portals, not 65 companies

The research landed and it shrank this lane by a factor of seven. All 65 property management companies were checked. 8 have a verified vendor portal, 1 is likely, and 56 have none at all.

Minutes per registration: 20 to 30, because they want a W-9, a certificate of insurance, the license number and sometimes a fee. A 90 minute block holds about 3, so this whole lane is roughly 3 blocks of work and then it is done.

Ungated: it needs no contact name, so it is the one lane the 87 placeholders do not block. It is also the only lane that can be finished. See section 12.

Follow-up block grows from everything above

Whatever came due. Empty at the start of the program and never empty again after the second week of work. Budget for it before it exists, because the single most common way a campaign like this dies is that the first touches go out, the follow-ups never do, and the list looks worked when it is not.

5. The dependency rules

These eight constrain what order things can happen in. They do not tell you which day to do them, and section 6 explains why that is deliberately your call.

1
No email to a record that still shows a role placeholder.
All 87 of them. There is no person to address. This is the hard gate and it is the reason the call block runs ahead of the email block.
2
A walk-in follows a call on the same record. It never precedes one.
You get one first impression per building. Spend it knowing who you are asking for.
3
A follow-up trails its own first touch. Always.
Obvious, and it is the rule most often broken by a calendar that says 'follow-up Thursday' with nothing behind it. The follow-up block works a list, not a slot.
4
Verified phones before secondary phones, inside every call block.
68 verified against 39 unconfirmed. Front-loading the confirmed numbers is what keeps early blocks producing conversations.
5
Shared-reach records are dialed together, in one call.
Nine numbers, emails and offices are shared across fourteen records. One dial reaches two. Name both properties on that call so the person on the other end hears the whole ask once.
6
The portal lane runs in parallel and is never blocked.
It needs no contact name, so it does not wait on the call block. It is also the slowest to produce anything, which is exactly why it should start earliest.
7
A leave-behind is handed over, not mailed.
It goes in the truck for walk-ins and gets sent after a call that went somewhere. It is not a cold-open piece.
8
Every touch gets recorded the same day it happens.
Not the same week. Section 7 covers where. An unrecorded call is a call you will make again by accident, to someone who remembers you.

7. Where a touch gets recorded

Right now, there is nowhere to put it.

The fields this cadence needs - calledDate, emailedDate, mailedDate, walkedInDate, respondedOnTouch, commercialTier - do not exist in Contractors Cloud, and they cannot be created through the API. Custom fields on this account are web-app only. The only two custom fields that exist today are Date Of Loss and Preferred Contact Method, neither of which fits.

A cadence nobody can measure is a wish. So this gets solved before the first call block, not after.

The two options, honestly

Option 1: create six custom fields by hand

What it costs: one person, one sitting, in the Contractors Cloud web app. Six fields.

What it buys: a filterable list view. "Show me every Commercial Outbound record with no calledDate" is one click, and that is the exact question the call block asks at the start of every session.

What it does not buy: context. A date tells you a call happened. It does not tell you what was said.

Option 2: record every touch as a dated typed note

What it costs: nothing to set up. Notes post through the API today, with a type from the existing catalog: Call - Outbound, Email - Outbound, Follow Up, Appointment, Text (SMS) - Outgoing.

What it buys: the full record of what happened, permanently, with a type you can filter on.

What it does not buy: a "who have I not called yet" view. To answer that you have to open 131 projects and read. Notes are also immutable - a note posted wrong cannot be edited, only deleted and re-posted.

The recommendation: do both, because they answer different questions

The six custom fields are the system of record for STATE. They answer "what do I do next," and state has to be filterable in one view or the cadence has no starting line each session.

Typed notes are the system of record for CONTENT. They answer "what happened," and they are the only place the actual conversation survives.

If only one of the two ever gets built, build the fields. Notes without fields still leave you reading 131 records to find out where you are. Fields without notes still tell you where you are. The fields are roughly a ten minute job and they are the highest-leverage ten minutes in this whole document.

8. Automation: Zapier, Claude, or neither

The finding that decides this, checked today

Contractors Cloud does have a native Zapier app. It is listed at zapier.com under Contractors Cloud. But the only action it publishes is Create Lead, and no trigger could be found - nothing that fires a Zap when something happens inside Contractors Cloud.

That matters more than anything else on this page about automation. The CRM cannot be the trigger. "Guy logs a call and a Zap sends the email" is not buildable on the native app. The trigger has to come from somewhere else, which means a second place where the work gets typed in.

Not confirmed: whether the app exposes any trigger not listed on its public page, or any action beyond Create Lead.

The third answer, which is the real one: automate the timer and the record. Never the message.

There are 78 email addresses on this entire list, and the gate in section 1 means they do not all become sendable at once. At 10 per email block, this is a handful of emails a week for a couple of months. A normal Google Workspace account will send 2,000 messages a day, so volume is not the constraint and never will be. Automation here would be solving a problem this campaign does not have.

Worse, it would solve it badly. A cold note to a property manager who has never heard of Top Tier works because it reads like one person wrote it to one company, referencing the specific buildings they own. Every one of these 131 records already carries a research note with exactly that detail on it. The moment that becomes a merge field, the only thing making the email work is gone.

Automate the two things humans genuinely fail at:

  1. The follow-up timer. Nobody forgets to send the first email. Everybody forgets the second. Delay by Zapier does this, and so does a calendar reminder, and so does Claude when asked what is due.
  2. The logging. Not because it is hard, because it is boring and it is the first thing dropped when a day goes sideways. This is the one place the API is worth wiring up.

Do not automate: the sending, the wording, or the decision about who gets touched next. At this volume those are cheap by hand and expensive to get wrong.

9. The email lane, and what the law requires of it

CAN-SPAM applies to this. All of it.

Straight from the FTC's own compliance guide: "Despite its name, the CAN-SPAM Act doesn't apply just to bulk email. It covers all commercial messages... The law makes no exception for business-to-business email."

So a single note typed by hand to one property manager is covered exactly the same as a blast to ten thousand. Penalties run up to $53,088 per individual email, which is the figure the FTC currently publishes and which is adjusted for inflation annually.

The seven requirements

#RequirementWhat it means for these emails
1No false or misleading header informationFrom, Reply-To and routing must be accurate and identify the sender. Send from Guy's real address.
2No deceptive subject linesThe subject has to reflect what is actually in the message. "Re:" on a first contact is a violation.
3Identify the message as an adClear and conspicuous. It does not have to be ugly, but a solicitation has to read as one.
4Tell recipients where you are locatedA valid physical postal address in every message. Top Tier's is on the template below.
5Tell recipients how to opt outA clear way to stop receiving marketing email. A reply address counts.
6Honor opt-outs promptlyWithin 10 business days. The mechanism has to keep working for at least 30 days after the message goes out. No fee, no extra information demanded, no more than a single reply or one web page.
7Monitor what others do on your behalfHiring it out does not transfer the liability. If a vendor sends on Top Tier's behalf, Top Tier is still on the hook.

And one Minnesota requirement on top

Minnesota Statute 326B.87 requires the contractor license number on every piece of advertising. A cold commercial email is advertising. Top Tier's license is BC807923 and it belongs in the signature block of every message in this lane, along with the mail piece and anything left behind at a door.

What 3 a week actually changes
