What we are computing, in one paragraph

Customer acquisition cost is the money you spend to get one signed contract. Take everything you spent on getting work in a month. Divide by the number of contracts you signed that month. That is the whole formula. The reason it is not on a report anywhere is that 159 of your 161 project records say the lead came from a referral, so the system cannot tell you which dollar produced which job. Contractors Cloud

Three numbers come out of it, and they answer three different questions.

NumberThe question it answers
Cost per leadIs the phone ringing for a sensible price?
Cost per appointmentAre you driving out to the right people, or wasting Guy's day?
Cost per signed jobDoes the roof pay for the customer who bought it? This is the one that matters.
The rule that makes all of this real
"Referral" is only allowed as a lead source when you can type a human being's name next to it. Everything else picks a real value. That one habit, on the call, is what turns this worksheet from a one time exercise into a number you can pull every quarter without anybody's help.

Why payback is counted in jobs and never in weeks
A roofing month in Minnesota is not like the month before it. Counting payback in time means guessing at weather. Counting it in roofs means one honest sentence: this program is paid for on the day the Nth extra roof is signed. No calendar required, and nobody has to be right about the spring.

The capacity wall

The honest ceiling on the whole growth plan
Past the wall, the economics keep looking good on paper and stop working in the truck. The next dollar buys a lead that rings out. Worse than wasted, in the Google Local Services auction a missed call lowers your ranking, so an unanswered lead makes the next lead more expensive. The wall moves only when somebody other than Guy reliably answers the phone. A hire, an answering service or an AI receptionist, and the cost of it belongs inside the marketing budget, not outside it.
