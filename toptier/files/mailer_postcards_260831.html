Top Tier homeowner mailers

Four creative treatments, four trim sizes. The front is Top Tier. The back comes in two complete versions: one that gives everything which is not postal to a local licensed insurance agent, and one with no agent on it at all. Everything below is print ready artwork at 300 dpi with a 0.125 in bleed and a 0.125 in safety margin. The dashed red and navy lines you can see are proof guides that sit outside the exported artwork and never print. 39 faces: 13 fronts, 13 variant D backs and 13 variant T backs.

Treatment A is the lead piece and 4.25 x 6 is the size that mails, to 1,433 homes as addressed First-Class postcards. Everything else here is a spare.

Pick where the phone number goes

The number used to print twice on the same face. Top Tier’s logo artwork has the phone number and the license number baked into the raster, underneath the wordmark, and a previous pass then set the number again as live type. So the card said 612-205-5503 in the logo and (612) 205-5503 in the call to action, six inches apart.

The logo is now cropped to the wordmark and the number is set once, as type. The crop falls in the gap under “Roofing & Remodeling LLC.”, which is a real break in the lockup rather than a slice through it, and it leaves the mark balanced: the original had 172 px of clear space above the roof and the crop leaves 170 px below the descriptor. What is lost from the raster is the phone number, which we did not want twice, and the license number, which is live text in the license bar on every face and always was. Nothing that has to print has gone missing.

Three placements, same card, so the choice is made by looking. One of them is live on all thirteen fronts and is labelled. Say which one you want and it is a one word change that restyles every front at once.

The number is read second, straight after the line that earns the card its attention, and the foot of the card stays clean: mark, quote, approved line, identity, map. Nothing competes with the quote.

The footer reads left to right as who we are, how to reach us, where we are. Quietest of the three and the most designed. The number is display size but it is not shouting, which suits a card whose argument is a citation.

Loudest and the most conventional direct mail. The number is the last thing read and it is unmissable. The cost is a fourth stacked element at the bottom of a card that already carries the approved line, the identity bar and the map.

Read this before anything goes to a printer

  • His name is Brant, not Brandt, and that is settled. The brief said “Derek Brandt”. Minnesota’s own licensing database was swept for the surname BRANDT with the first name left blank: all 91 records came back and there is no Derek Brandt, no Derrick, no Derik, anywhere in Minnesota, non-residents licensed here included. Meanwhile BRANT, DEREK JON is licensed at the exact street address and phone of the agency five miles from the shop, serving Top Tier’s exact town list. One candidate, and he checks out.
  • License verified, and property and casualty is what he holds. MN license 40194133, NPN 15656867, Resident Insurance Producer, active, original issue 2010-02-08, expires 2027-01-31. Property, Casualty, Life, Accident and Health, and Variable Life and Variable Annuities are all active. Source: the State of Minnesota consumer license inquiry. A number circulating online as “MN-40293488” is wrong and appears in no primary source. Do not use it.
  • He is a captive State Farm agent, not an independent. This is the one thing that can stop the drop. His carrier almost certainly requires advertising compliance review before his name appears on a contractor’s mailer, and carrier brand rules commonly prohibit contractor co-branding outright. That approval is his to get, not ours. Nothing prints with his panel until he has it in writing.
  • No carrier logo appears anywhere on any piece, by design. The only published mark he has is the State Farm sign on his building, which is the carrier’s trademark and cannot go on a roofer’s mail piece. So his logo slot is a marked placeholder and the agency name is a fill-in. What does print is his own name, his own license and his own phone, none of which are State Farm’s property.
  • There are two complete backs, not one back with a removable corner. He is no longer a panel on Top Tier’s back, he is the whole back, so a refusal would not leave a hole, it would leave no card. Every size therefore ships as variant D, his back, and variant T, a Top Tier only back that is print complete on its own and mails unchanged if the approval never comes. Neither is a sketch. Send a printer the wrong one and you get a different card, not a broken one.
  • His logo slot is a stub and the card says so. The box reads “Agent logo” and states its own printed size. It deliberately does not print the words “pending State Farm approval”, because putting the carrier’s name on a roofing contractor’s mail piece is the exact thing that is not ours to do. The approval is real and it is pending: it belongs in this note and in the spec view, not on the artwork.
  • The pitch in his panel is a draft and is marked as one on the card. It is written from what he publishes about himself, but it is his license on the line and every syllable is his to rewrite.

Why the copy is split the way it is

The split is not a design choice, it is the compliance structure made visible. A Minnesota roofing contractor may not interpret policy provisions or advise on coverage (325E.66 subd. 1(a)(3), up to $10,000 per violation), may not advertise that it will pay or waive a deductible (325E.66 subd. 1(a)(1)), and may not advertise itself as a public adjuster (72B.02 subd. 6(2), where the advertisement is itself the regulated act). A licensed producer may say all of those things. So Top Tier’s half of every card talks about shingles, water, age and wood, and the agent’s half talks about insurance in his own name, under his own license.

  • The only insurance adjacent sentences in Top Tier’s voice anywhere in this file are the two approved verbatim: “We can’t help you with your policy, but we can help you with your roof.” That is the only one, and it is Dale’s approved wording set verbatim. An earlier draft also carried a sentence about meeting an adjuster on the roof. It has been cut from every face: it is first person insurance language in the contractor’s voice, which is exactly what 325E.66 subd. 1(a)(3) reaches.
  • Treatment A carries the Insurance Federation of Minnesota sentence verbatim and unaltered, in quotation marks, with the full citation set directly under it, on every one of its four sizes. Repeating what insurers told lawmakers is reporting. Telling a homeowner what their own policy does would not be, and no card does it. The citation is not decoration: it is the thing that makes the sentence reporting rather than advice.
  • Nothing anywhere says or implies that insurers are dropping people over roof age. Non-renewal on that basis is not a thing in Minnesota and it is not on any piece.
  • MN Statute 326B.87 requires the contractor license number on every piece of advertising. BC807923 appears on every front, twice over, because Top Tier’s own logo artwork carries it as well as the license bar. It also appears on every variant T back. It does not appear on a variant D back, because that face is the agent’s and the advertisement carries the number on its front.
  • No money, referral or payment language appears on any piece. If Top Tier and the agent share the cost of the drop, that is a marketing service rendered and it lives in an invoice, never on the card.

Bleed is 0.125 in on every edge, safety margin a further 0.125 in inside the trim. All four trims are landscape so that length divided by height lands between 1.3 and 2.5, which is what USPS wants for machinable mail. The renderer writes a 300 dpi PNG and a vector PDF sized to the bleed canvas for every face. Send the printer the PDF.

What each size actually costs to mail, and why.

  • 6 x 4.25 in → postcard, $0.65 retail. Sits exactly ON the retail card maximum of 4-1/4 in high by 6 in long (DMM 101.6.2.2). Aspect 1.41, inside the 1.3 to 2.5 machinability band. The 0.009 in minimum stock rule does not bite, because it applies to cards MORE than 4-1/4 high or 6 long and this is equal to, not more than.
  • 9 x 6 in → letter, $0.65 commercial card, $0.82 retail. The trap in this set. At 9 in long and 6 in high it is under both letter maximums (11-1/2 long, 6-1/8 high) so it stays letter-size and does NOT become a flat. It hits the COMMERCIAL card maximum exactly (DMM 201.1.2.1) so a commercial mailing gets card price, but over the retail counter it busts the 4-1/4 by 6 retail card maximum and pays letter postage. Stock must be at least 0.009 in thick or it drops to nonmachinable and picks up the $0.49 surcharge.
  • 9 x 6.5 in → flat, $0.260 EDDM Retail. 6.5 in exceeds the 6-1/8 in letter height maximum, which under DMM 201.4.1 is exactly what makes a piece a flat. That is the whole point of the half inch: it buys EDDM Retail eligibility at 26 cents a piece. Well inside the flat maximum of 15 by 12 by 3/4 in. The aspect ratio rule does not apply to flats.
  • 12 x 9 in → flat, $0.260 EDDM Retail. Length is the longest dimension, so this is 12 in long by 9 in high (DMM 201.4.2). 9 in is well over the 6-1/8 in letter height maximum, so it is a flat, and it is inside the 15 by 12 by 3/4 in flat maximum. Same 26 cent EDDM Retail price as the 6.5 x 9, on four times the paper.

Two of these look nearly identical on a desk and are in different mail classes: 6 x 9 is a letter, 6.5 x 9 is a flat. The half inch of height is what buys EDDM Retail eligibility at 26 cents a piece, because a piece becomes a flat the moment it exceeds 6-1/8 in high.

EDDM lost, and the drop goes out as addressed First-Class postcards. EDDM Retail is cheaper per piece and far more expensive per target. Covering the mailable list through EDDM means buying whole carrier routes: 10,552 pieces at $0.260 to reach 1,433 target homes, against 1,433 pieces at $0.650 addressed. That is 2.95 times the postage for the same 1,433 homes. Break even needs 40 percent of a route to be on the list and the best route available is 29.3 percent. EDDM also has to be a flat, not a postcard, so it would have forced the more expensive printing as well. So 4.25 x 6 is the primary size and the one to proof first. The other three stay in this file for flexibility, for handouts, and for door hangers, not for the drop.

Verified 2026-08-31 against the Domestic Mail Manual, section 201, at pe.usps.com/text/dmm300/201.htm, and the Notice 123 price list effective 2026-07-12. EDDM Retail also carries a floor of 200 pieces and a ceiling of 5,000 per ZIP per day.

The postal areas are reserved by the layout, not by a dashed line. The address side is a grid whose right hand column and bottom strip are the recipient address block, the postage area and the barcode clear zone. Artwork occupies one cell and has nowhere to spill, which is a stronger promise than a guide drawn on top of a full bleed layout. The reserve is measured from the trim edge, because that is the edge USPS measures from. Top Tier’s return address does print, because it is real, and it is set as postal furniture in the upper left rather than as a brand element.

Two lines that have to appear on the print order itself, not in a plan.

  1. Code the pieces by city on this first drop. A short city code set in the corner of the address side, or a distinct response number or landing address per city, whichever the printer finds cheaper. It costs nothing extra at the printer and it is the only way to read St Michael's response separately from Buffalo, Monticello and Otsego. Those are two different kinds of list and one mailing will say which one pays.
  2. The mail house validates every carrier route code against USPS before any presort or automation discount is claimed. The codes on the supplied file were assigned by proximity to the nearest published route centerline, not looked up, so they are an inference and some will be wrong. The mail house holds the address-matching software that settles it. Pieces presorted on wrong codes get kicked back or re-rated and the saving disappears, so this is a line item on the order and a number they confirm, never an assumption.

Who the 1,433 pieces go to, and why the city code matters. 1,913 candidate parcels less the 480 that had already pulled a re-roof permit dated 2012 or later. Of the 1,433 that remain, 491 are in St Michael, whose complete permit index 2002 to 2026 was searched house by house, and 942 are in Buffalo, Monticello and Otsego, which publish no useful roof permits and whose roof age is estimated from the year the house was built. In St Michael, the one city where it can be measured, 478 of 969 candidates, 49.3 percent, already had a permitted re-roof. A meaningful share of the 942 will land on a roof that is already new, which is ordinary for cold direct mail and is exactly why the two halves are coded and counted apart.

The postal areas, and why they are now part of the layout

This was a defect and it was on all thirteen backs, at every size. The address side used to be a full bleed layout with two dashed keep clear boxes drawn on top of it. A box drawn on top of a layout is advice, and copy does not read advice. Measured in the browser, 104 elements were sitting inside a keep clear area: the return address ran under the postage box by its full 1.375 in width on every size, and the license bar ran straight through the recipient address block, by 2.52 x 0.34 in on the 4.25 x 6 and by 4.92 x 0.69 in on the 9 x 12, taking the phone number, the web address and the town list with it. The mail house prints the address and the indicia into exactly those rectangles, so every one of those elements would have been printed over, and a piece whose address block is not clear can be surcharged or rejected.

The fix is structural rather than cosmetic. The back is now a CSS grid whose second column and second row are the postal areas. Artwork lives in one cell. There is no cell under the address block for anything to flow into, so the failure cannot recur the next time a line of copy grows. A test measures every element on every face in the browser and fails the build if any bounding box intersects any postal rectangle. It is at work/scripts/check_postal_clearance_260831.py and it currently reports zero.

The rectangles were re-derived from USPS rather than carried forward. Verified 2026-08-31 against the Domestic Mail Manual as updated 2026-07-12, at pe.usps.com. What changed, and what turned out to be folklore:

  • The barcode clear zone was missing entirely and it is the one people forget. DMM 202.5.1.1 defines it as 4-3/4 in from the right edge by 5/8 in up from the bottom edge, on the address side of cards and letter-size pieces. The old file had no such zone at all, and the license bar sat in it. It is now reserved full width, because on a 6 in long card the 1-1/4 in that falls outside it is not worth having.
  • Flats do not have one, so the two flats give the space back. DMM 202.5.2.1 lets the Intelligent Mail barcode sit anywhere on the address side of a flat provided it is at least 1/8 in from an edge. So the 6.5 x 9 and the 9 x 12 reserve 1/4 in along the bottom instead of 5/8 in. Over-reserving is not free when the whole point is to give the agent as much of the face as we can.
  • The 6 x 9 is a letter, not a flat, so it keeps the full letter geometry. DMM 201.1.1.1b caps a letter at 11-1/2 by 6-1/8 in and a 6 x 9 is inside both. It also hits the card price maximum exactly at 9 long by 6 high (DMM 201.1.2.1c). Stock has to land between 0.009 and 0.016 in thick to hold that price.
  • The address reserve follows the OCR read area on the two letter-size pieces. DMM 202.2.1 places it 5/8 to 2-3/4 in up from the bottom edge, 1/2 in in from each side, with Publication 25 section 1-3.2 preferring 3/4 in on the right for lead edge fold over. That is a recommended placement, not a mandatory blank rectangle: Publication 28 Appendix A1 expressly contemplates other printing in the read area and asks only that it sit above the delivery address line and as far from the address block as possible. Which is exactly what the left hand content column now does.
  • The postage reserve is derived, because USPS publishes no indicium size. DMM 604.4.3.3 requires 1/4 in from the top edge, 1/4 in from the right edge, and a 1/2 in clear zone to the left of and below every element of the indicium. A permit imprint (DMM 604.5.3.4) carries no dimensions at all. The reserve here is a normal indicium plus that clearance, and it is deliberately generous.
  • Numbers we did not use, because they are not in any USPS document. A 1.5 x 1.5 in or 2.5 x 1.5 in postage box, a return address limited to half the length of the piece, an address area 3/4 in up and 2-1/8 in tall. All of these circulate on commercial printer websites. None of them appear in the DMM or in Publication 25 or 28.
  • One thing to ask the mail house before this prints. DMM 202.5.1.1 waives the barcode clear zone entirely if the piece carries an Intelligent Mail barcode with a delivery point routing code inside the address block, which is what most houses do. If ours does, the bottom 5/8 in comes back and the 4.25 x 6 gets meaningfully roomier. We reserve it because we do not control that choice yet. Separately, and this is press practice rather than a postal rule: an inkjet addressed piece cannot carry UV coating on the address side.

Nothing here is waiting on a photographer

Every place a photograph belongs is a stub: a pale box with a dashed amber outline that states, on the artwork itself, what the shot is, how big it prints, and the minimum pixel count that shot has to be to survive 300 dpi at that size. Hand a proof to whoever is holding the camera and they do not have to ask a single question. A stub cannot be mistaken for a design element, and it is not a black box, so it will not eat a printer’s ink if one of these proofs gets run by mistake.

  • There is no photo stub on any back, and that is a deliberate cut. Once the message, the call to action and the postal reserve had taken their room, the slot left over was a sliver between 3.6 and 4.9 wide to 1 high. A sliver of a roof is decoration rather than proof of work. Cutting it also takes photography off the critical path: nothing in this file is now waiting on somebody shooting a completed roof in good light, so the drop can go to the printer as soon as the agent’s carrier answers.
  • The Top Tier logo is NOT a stub. It is real artwork, in hand at 12000 x 7200 px, and it already carries the phone number and BC807923. It is placed, not reserved. It appears on every front and on every variant T back. It no longer carries the phone number or the license number: the raster is cropped to the wordmark so the number prints once, as type, and BC807923 is live text in the license bar.
  • Derek Brant’s logo IS a stub, sized for a real agency mark. His agency mark goes in it. The carrier mark never does.
  • Treatment A’s front has no photograph, and that is the design, not an oversight. The minimal treatment was approved by name. That face carries the roofline and the numeral, the quote, the phone number, the locator map and the license bar. The map is drawn artwork rather than a photograph, and it is set as corner furniture at about a fifth of the width so it anchors rather than competes. Its spec view shows every zone on that face and shows that none of them is an image.

What the spec view next to every card is for

Each finished face is printed next to a placement map of itself: the same card with every zone outlined, numbered and dimensioned in inches, measured from the bleed edge, which is the top left corner of the artwork file. Trim, bleed and safety are all drawn. A printer or a designer can rebuild any of these from the map alone, and the table under each map says which zones are fixed and which are open to rewrite.

The maps are page furniture and they never print. They are siblings of the artwork element, not part of it. The renderer promotes the artwork element to be the only node in the document before it screenshots or prints it, so a map is structurally incapable of reaching an exported PNG or PDF. That has been verified against the exported files rather than assumed.

The postal reserve is drawn on every back map, and it is structural. The address block, the postage area and the barcode clear zone are not guides sitting on top of the artwork any more, they are grid cells that the artwork has no way to enter. What the map shows and what the layout enforces are the same rectangles, taken from the same numbers.

How to read the spec view on the right of every card: Fixed. Logo, license number, the quote, the attribution, the approved line. Do not touch. Swappable. Headlines and body copy. Rewrite freely. Image stub. Nothing shot yet. The box states the subject and the minimum pixels. Deletable. Derek Brant’s panel. If his carrier declines, delete the block and the card still works. Postal keep clear. Left blank on purpose. The mail house places the address and the postage.

Treatment A: the 10 year insurance shift · the lead piece

Why this one works. The one piece of genuinely new information a homeowner in Wright County does not have. It works because it is reporting: the Insurance Federation of Minnesota said this in writing, to the legislature, on the record. Top Tier repeats what was said and stops. The agent panel is where the sentence a homeowner actually wants gets said, by the only person on the card licensed to say it.

What the front does, and it does one thing. The roof and the numeral 10, the quote, and nothing else. The mark is the one Dale picked out of the infographic set, rebuilt so it holds together at postcard size. The headline that used to sit above the quote is gone and so is the depreciation chart: they were both restating the quote, and the quote says it better and says it on the record. The quote is now the largest text block on the piece, which is the point.

Where the four required elements land. Front carries the mark, the quote with its citation, Dale’s approved line, and the Top Tier logo in the license bar. The back is his, entirely, on variant D: his mark, his message, his license number and his phone, with nothing of Top Tier’s on it but a plain return address. The split is not a space problem, it is the compliance structure: repeating what insurers told lawmakers is reporting and can sit in Top Tier’s space, and interpreting a policy can only happen in his.
