What we are computing, in one paragraph

Customer acquisition cost is the money you spend to get one signed contract. Take everything you spent on getting work in a month. Divide by the number of contracts you signed that month. That is the whole formula.

Three numbers come out of it, and they answer three different questions.

Swipe the table sideways to see every column.

NumberThe question it answers

Cost per signed jobDoes the roof pay for the customer who bought it? This is the one that matters.
One habit makes this a number you can pull yourselves
Of the 87 signed contracts in your book, 82 came in through a referral. That is the book this company was built on, and a referral names itself at the door. The new channels are different: a mailer, a review, a map listing and an ad all arrive as a stranger on the phone, and nothing about that call says which one sent them. Picking a real value in the lead source field on those calls is what lets you pull a cost per job per channel next quarter without anybody's help. Contract book as of 2026-09-04.

Why payback is counted in jobs and never in weeks
A roofing month in Minnesota is not like the month before it. Counting payback in time means guessing at weather. Counting it in roofs means one plain sentence: this program is paid for on the day the Nth extra roof is signed. No calendar required.

The capacity wall

Where the ceiling actually sits
Answering the phone is not the constraint here: you catch every call today and have said you could take 10 leads a day. Your own capacity read puts sales at 100 against a capacity of 250, with sales circled as the constraint, and the same read puts operations in the same place. So the wall in this model is the number of leads that can be worked, and it moves when selling time does. Past the wall the economics keep looking good on paper and stop working in the truck: in the Google Local Services auction a missed call lowers your ranking, so an unanswered lead makes the next lead more expensive. What this worksheet cannot tell you yet, and what it costs

Four things are still missing. Each one is cheap to fix and each one turns a box on this page from an assumption into a measurement.

Swipe the table sideways to see every column.

What is missingWhy it is missingWhat changes when you have it
Which channel produced which job Your book is 82 referrals out of 87 contracts, and a referral names itself. The new channels will not. The whole table in section one turns on. Right now section four gives you one blended cost per job. With a real lead source on the new calls you get a cost per job per program, and you can move money by name instead of by feel. Cost: one field at the moment the record is created.
What a job actually cost 8 of 87 contracts carry a real bill in the CRM. Margin per job, per city, per crew and per type, generated by the system with nobody computing it by hand. On the 8 jobs that do carry bills the measured margin runs 19.94% to 62.17%, which is the spread the average on this page hides. Cost: two entries per job, the supplier bill and the customer payment.
The marketing that is not on the card Named card vendors come to $398.44 a month against $951 a month of advertising on the 2025 return. Every number in section four. If the difference turns out to be real marketing paid another way, a new program is partly a reallocation and costs less new money than it looks. If it is not marketing at all, the return's advertising line needs a different home. That is the difference between an easy decision and a hard one.
The result of a site visit All 24 appointment records covering 2026-04-18 to 2026-08-17 have a blank result field. The middle box in section four, cost per site visit, becomes real rather than assumed, and the gap between a lead and a visit becomes visible. Cost: one dropdown after the visit.
