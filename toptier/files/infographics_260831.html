The eave

Where the water goes.

BEFORE Metal tight to the fascia
AFTER Metal stands off and discharges over the trough

IRC R905.2.8.5, adopted in Minnesota Rules chapter 1309. Drip edge is required at eaves and rakes. Adjacent pieces lap not less than 2 inches. The metal extends not less than 1/4 inch below the sheathing and not less than 2 inches back onto the deck, fastened not more than 12 inches on center. Underlayment goes over the drip edge at the eave and under it at the rake.

Synthetic underlayment syntheticUnderlayment · technical · comparison 1080 × 1350 portrait

Under the shingles

The layer you
never see again.

Underlayment is the only thing between your deck and the weather while a roof is open, and the backup after it is closed. There are two kinds and they do not behave the same.

FELT
  • Tears under a boot or a gust, and 15 pound felt is the worst of it.
  • Wrinkles and buckles if it gets rained on before the shingles go down.
  • Has to be covered fast. Days, not weeks.
SYNTHETIC
  • Woven polypropylene. It does not tear the way felt does.
  • Lies flat and stays flat if the weather turns mid job.
  • Textured surface, so the crew has footing on your roof.

Source: Owens Corning published product data for ProArmor synthetic underlayment, which carries a manufacturer rating of up to 90 days of exposure, and Owens Corning's own synthetic against felt comparison. Exposure ratings differ by product, so the number on your roof is whatever that roll is rated for. Underlayment itself is required by IRC R905.2.7.

Kick-out flashing kickOutFlashing · technical · before/after 1080 × 1350 portrait

The one everybody skips

One missing piece
rots the wall.

BEFORE Step flashing just ends at the corner
AFTER A kick-out turns the water into the gutter

IRC R903.2.1: a flashing must be installed to divert water away from where the eave of a sloped roof meets a vertical sidewall. Metal flashing must be corrosion resistant and not less than 0.019 inch thick. The code has carried this requirement since 2009 without using the trade name for the part.

Back caulking a penetration backCaulkPenetration · technical · before/after 1080 × 1350 portrait

Bath fans, kitchen vents, pipe boots

Water runs down.
So does the flange.

Looking straight down. Water runs to the bottom.

BEFORE Flange laid on top, caulked at the front
AFTER Shingled in, then sealed on the back and sides

IRC R905.2.8.4 requires soil stack, vent pipe and chimney flashing to be applied according to the shingle manufacturer's printed instructions, and every one of them specifies the flange woven into the courses: uphill half under, downhill half over. Sealing the back and sides is added practice, not a code minimum. We do it anyway, because the sealant then lives under a shingle instead of baking in the sun.

Ice and water shield placement iceWaterPenetrations · technical · code against practice 1080 × 1350 portrait

Ice and water shield

The code says
the eaves. That is
the minimum.

IRC R905.1.2, adopted in Minnesota Rules chapter 1309, requires an ice barrier at the eaves running from the lowest edge of the roof to a point not less than 24 inches inside the exterior wall line, and 36 inches along the slope where the pitch is 8:12 or steeper. Everything else shown here is manufacturer recommendation, not code: Owens Corning specifies its WeatherLock membrane for eaves, valleys, vents, chimneys and skylights.
