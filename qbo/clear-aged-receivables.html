Clear Out Aged Accounts Receivable

Working the aging report: apply what is already there, collect what is live, and write off what is dead without deleting anything.
QBO Wizard  |  Written for an owner doing this alone, with no bookkeeping background

What this job is, in plain words

Accounts receivable is the money customers owe you and have not paid. An aging report is that money sorted by how old it is: due now, 30 days late, 60, 90, older. Aged receivables are the ones that have been sitting there long enough to be a problem.

Working this list is the highest paid hour in the business, because some of it is money you can still collect and some of it was never really owed at all. You do not find out which is which by looking. You find out by going through it in the order below.

Before you start

  • What you need: a computer, two browser tabs, and a couple of uninterrupted hours. This is not a five minute job and pretending otherwise is why it never gets done.
  • A QuickBooks login allowed to create credit memos and receive payments. A sales only or reports only login cannot finish this, and only a company admin can widen it.
  • The authority to give up on money. Writing a receivable off is a business decision, not a bookkeeping chore. If you are the owner, that is yours to make. If you are not, get the owner's yes in writing before Step 8.
  • An accountant you can email. Two things here depend on how your books are kept and which tax years are already filed. Both are flagged in the steps. One short email answers both, and it is worth sending before you start rather than half way through.
  • The bank feed caught up. A payment that arrived but was never recorded looks exactly like an unpaid invoice, so clear that queue first: Match downloaded bank and credit card transactions.
  • Time: 30 minutes on a tidy ledger. A first pass through several years of build up is a day, and most of it is Steps 4 to 6.

Read this first. One of these is genuinely irreversible.

  • Never delete an old invoice to make it go away. Deleting removes income from a period you have probably already reported and paid tax on, changes financial statements you may have already given a bank, and destroys the record of what the work was. Write it off instead, which leaves everything visible. See Step 8.
    If you have already deleted invoices: QuickBooks keeps a history of who did what, under the gear icon in the audit log, and a deleted transaction's details are usually still readable there. You can re-enter it from that record. Do this sooner rather than later, and do not delete any more while you work out the damage.
  • Never write anything off before Step 4. A large share of old balances are not unpaid at all. They are payments and credits sitting unapplied, meaning the money came in but was never attached to the invoice it paid. Writing those off destroys real money in your books and leaves the customer's account wrong in the other direction.
  • Do not touch a tax year that has already been filed without asking. Dating a write off inside a filed year changes numbers that were reported to the government. One email to your accountant settles which date to use. See Step 8.
  • How you write off depends on whether your books are kept on a cash or an accrual basis. On accrual, income is counted when you invoice, so it was already counted and writing it off gives you a bad debt expense. On cash, income is only counted when the money arrives, so it was never counted, and a write off that creates an expense counts the loss twice. Unconfirmed for your file: ask your accountant which basis you are on before Step 8. It is a one line question.

The steps

1Sign in to QuickBooks Online

Open a browser and go to:

https://qbo.intuit.com
2Run the aging report and keep a copy
  1. Left menu Reports.
  2. In the search box at the top, type Accounts receivable aging detail and open it. The detail version lists every single invoice. The summary version gives one line per customer, which is useful to look at but useless for the work you are about to do.
  3. Set the report date to the date you are working as of. Today is usually right.
  4. Export it to a spreadsheet or save the PDF.

Type the total and the over 90 figure into the boxes above before you go on.

Why keep the copy: this is your before picture. In Step 9 it is the only way to see what your afternoon actually achieved.
Looks right: columns for current, 1 to 30, 31 to 60, 61 to 90 and 91 and over, with a total equal to the total owed to you.
3Sort the list before you work it

Do not start at the top. Work in this order, because it recovers the most money per hour of your time:

  1. Biggest balances first, regardless of age. A handful of customers is usually most of the money.
  2. Then anything over 90 days, which is the over 90 total. The odds of collecting fall sharply with age, so old and large is the urgent corner.
  3. Then the small old stuff, which is mostly cleanup rather than collection.
4Find and apply unapplied payments first

An unapplied payment is money you received and recorded, but which was never attached to the invoice it was paying. The invoice therefore still looks unpaid. This step usually removes the most balance for the least work and it is the one people skip.

  1. Reports → search Open Invoices and run it. Payments recorded but never applied show up on this report as negative lines under the customer.
  2. For each customer showing both an open invoice and an unapplied payment, click + New → Customers → Receive payment.
  3. Pick the customer. Their open invoices list at the top, their unapplied credits and payments below.
  4. Tick the invoice, tick the credit or payment, and check the amount being applied equals the invoice. The remaining balance should fall to zero.
  5. Save.
Correct: the invoice flips to Paid and the customer's open balance falls. No new money was recorded, because none arrived. You attached money that was already there.
Do not type an amount into the payment field here. If you type rather than ticking an existing credit, you have just recorded the money arriving a second time. Check the total at the bottom before you save.
If you have already recorded a payment twice: the customer will show a credit balance, meaning your books think you owe them money. Open the extra payment from Sales → Customers → their transaction list, and delete the one that never corresponded to real money in the bank. If both correspond to real deposits, the customer genuinely overpaid and that is a refund or a credit against their next invoice, not a bookkeeping error.
5Apply the credit memos

A credit memo is a document that reduces what a customer owes, usually because you overbilled or they returned something. Issued but never applied, it leaves the invoice open and the credit dangling, so both sides of their account are wrong.

  1. Same screen: + New → Receive payment, pick the customer.
  2. In the credits section, tick the credit memo and tick the invoice it belongs against.
  3. Check the payment amount at the top is zero. You are not receiving money here, you are matching two documents that already exist.
  4. Save.

If your file is set to apply credits automatically this may already have happened. If it is not, doing it by hand is the only way, and it is worth asking your accountant whether to turn that setting on so next year's list is shorter.

6Kill the duplicates, by voiding not deleting

Look for the same customer, the same amount, dates within a few days. Duplicate invoices are common where one person billed from the field and another billed from the office.

  1. Open the one you are sure is the duplicate, usually the later one with no payment against it.
  2. Use the action menu at the bottom of the invoice and choose Void, not Delete.
  3. In the memo, write what it duplicates and the date you voided it.
Why void rather than delete: a voided invoice keeps its number, keeps the trail, and shows as zero. A deleted one vanishes, and a gap in your invoice numbers is a question nobody can answer two years later, including you.
The duplicate has a payment applied to it: stop. Unapplying and reapplying a payment across two invoices is easy to get half right and it moves real money on your books. This is one to hand to an accountant, or at minimum to do slowly with the bank statement open beside you so you can prove which payment was which.
7Collect what is actually collectible

What survives Steps 4 to 6 is real money genuinely owed. From here it is a collection job, not a bookkeeping one.

  1. Send a statement: Sales → Customers, select the customers, and use the statement action. A statement lists every open invoice at once, which answers the standard stall of "send me a copy".
  2. Then telephone. Email gets ignored, statements get filed, calls get answered. Ask one question: what date will it be paid.
  3. Write what they said in the invoice memo with the date of the call. That memo is visible to anyone who opens the invoice, and by next month you will not remember.
  4. Call again on the date they promised. The follow up is the whole trick. One call moves nothing. A pattern of calls moves you up the pile ahead of the people who do not call.
Why the order matters: nothing costs you credibility faster than demanding money for an invoice that was paid four months ago, which is exactly what Steps 4 to 6 prevent.
8Write off what is dead

Some of it will never be paid. The customer is gone, the business closed, or the amount is smaller than the cost of chasing it. Leaving it on the report forever means every report you produce overstates what you are owed, and you will make decisions on a number that is not real.

Before you start: if you are not the owner, get the owner's approval in writing. Either way, ask your accountant two things: whether your books are on a cash or accrual basis, and what date to use so that an already filed tax year is not disturbed.

The method: a credit memo against a bad debt item

  1. Make the account, once. Transactions → Chart of accounts → New. Account type Expenses, detail type Bad debts, name it Bad Debts. Skip this if it already exists.
  2. Make the item, once. Gear icon → Products and services → New, a non inventory service named Bad Debt, with its income account pointed at the Bad Debts expense account you just made. Leave the price at zero and leave it non taxable.
  3. Create the credit memo. + New → Customers → Credit memo. Pick the customer, choose the Bad Debt item, enter the amount being written off, and write "Bad debt" in the message with the invoice number it relates to.
  4. Apply it. + New → Receive payment, pick the customer, tick the invoice and tick the credit memo, confirm the payment amount is zero, save.

Unconfirmed QuickBooks Online also has a Write off invoices tool that does this in a batch. It appears in the accountant tools, so whether it is available to you depends on your access and how your file is set up. If you have an outside accountant on the file, it is quicker to ask them to run it than to do thirty of these by hand.

Correct: the invoice reads Paid or Closed, the customer's open balance falls, and the amount appears as a Bad Debts expense on your profit report for the date you used.
If you deleted invoices instead of writing them off: look under the gear icon for the audit log, which records every change with the original details. Re-enter what was deleted from that record, then write those invoices off properly. Do this before the year is filed if you possibly can, and tell your accountant what happened rather than hoping it goes unnoticed.
9Prove what changed
  1. Rerun Accounts receivable aging detail for the same as of date.
  2. Compare it with the copy you saved in Step 2. You started at the original total, with the over 90 figure in the oldest column.
  3. Run the Balance Sheet, which is the report of what the business owns and owes. Its Accounts Receivable line must equal the total on the aging report for the same date.
A good outcome: the total came down, the oldest column came down hardest, and every line still on the report is one you can explain in a sentence.
The two reports do not agree: something has been posted straight into Accounts Receivable without going through an invoice, usually a journal entry made by someone trying to tidy up. That is an accountant question and worth an hour of their time, because until those two agree, neither number can be given to a bank or a buyer.
10Stop it coming back

Aged receivables are a habit problem, not an accounting problem. Four habits fix it permanently, and all four are things one person can hold:

  • Invoice the day the work finishes. Every day of delay adds a day to the age and loses a day of memory on both sides.
  • Run the aging report weekly, not when it becomes a crisis. Fifteen minutes on a Monday.
  • Call at 31 days, every time. An account that gets chased on a schedule gets paid ahead of the ones that do not.
  • Turn on online payments if the fee is acceptable to you. The easier it is to pay you, the sooner you get paid.
